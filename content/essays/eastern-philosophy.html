How Can One Grasp Light?

In a reality divided by dichotomies and dualities, our consciousness stands on an imperceptible edge of perception, grasping at flickering impressions of essences, yet only emptiness remains constant. We oscillate between the boundaries of language and the boundless cacophony of pure present experience, which carves out our experience. Intertwined with this paradoxical nature is the cyclical dance of yin and yang. It's within this dance that I’ve found myself, caught between the fiery will to grasp control and the cool contentment of acceptance, guided by ancient Eastern wisdom.

Before learning the rhythm of this dance, I was an equation attempting to solve itself, a question mark inscribed within the very fabric of existence, a mirror reflecting itself. With the reflections of meditation, the insight of Zen kōans, the centering of Śūnyatā, and the harmonious principles of yin and yang, I began to crystallize an acute awareness that I was not merely an abstract concept to define or an isolated fragment to put in a puzzle but a living, breathing reality.

This learning was not without its struggles. The same language that carved my mind’s pathways to explore and experience life also served to keep me in bondage. The reverberations of the Brahman-Atman intertwined with the infinite Tao, echoed in my mind, illuminating pathways toward a greater harmony that transcends mere definitions, equations, or reductionist constructs.

I will explore the rich tapestry of Eastern philosophy, weaving it with my personal experiences and Western exposure, examining the boundaries of language, and unraveling the transformative and paradoxical interactions between duality and non-duality. In the heart of the ineffable, where profound spiritual insights are confined within the finite framework of words, I will lift myself with the wisdom of Eastern giants and transcend the limitations of mere symbols, language, self, and more.

In the words of Chan Master I-tuan, “Speech is blasphemy, silence a lie. Above speech and silence, there is a way out” (I-tuan). As we embark on this exploration, may we uncover not just the wisdom of the East but universal truths that resonate with us all, as they have with me.

Where the Rock Meets the Ripple

Language, akin to a weed, roots deeply in our psyche. Familiarity often harbors danger. Our comprehension of language, resembling stagnant water, can lead to unnoticed ramifications and traps of assumptive thinking. The very bridges that we use to connect ideas, the colors and shapes we cast upon our images, and the vantage points from which we observe reality can become blurred, obscured by the habitual comfort of our chosen perspectives. Heidegger likens language to a “house of being” (Schonfeld). By containing ‘being’ within a system, we reduce the perfect system in which it was already contained.

When we throw a stone into a lake, we identify ourselves with the hand that releases it, not the stone, the ripples, or the intricate dance between them. Why is this distinction drawn, and what purpose does it serve? These distinctions, tendrils of the linguistic weed, reveal themselves early and gradually, taking form through words like 'I,' 'we,' 'other,' 'self,' 'hunger,' 'mother,' and 'father.’ To paint a beautiful masterpiece, one must start with a single stroke, and the illusory self emerges similarly. This flaw of language, encoding reality into distinct and decipherable meanings, is not conditioned solely by culture and teachings; it stems from our very genetic programming (Kaszniak).

Language has always been a measuring stick, and sanity check for the lens through which I view the world. If something is articulable, then the articulator must understand it. If it is not, then clearly they do not. I held all, including myself, to this standard. In this arrogant juxtaposition of prescribing singular understanding as a constant of reality, all greater real meanings shrink to fit within the measure of its bondage. Through this personal flaw, I have felt the effects of language’s failures and its tangible boundaries. The casting of my illusory self to my many selves, the overconfidence of understanding another’s view, the conceit of believing my understanding is singularly correct, and to the heart of duality, the conflation between the Tao and its parts.

Yin-yang is widely recognized as an icon of duality. It symbolizes embracing opposites and the harmonious interaction of forces. But many, including myself, fall into the trap of superficial understanding that language is prone to, even the language of visual expression. It embodies duality, yet it also represents non-duality; the little dots are not of arbitrary choice, and the cyclical implication of its shape is not a coincidence. Just as the top of a wheel becomes the bottom, and the bottom of a wheel becomes the top as it travels in motion; so too does the interplay of duality and non-duality. But how do we encapsulate this fluid dance using the stationary steps of calculative logic? To encode vibrancy within static symbols? Ultimately, it is not language alone that is to blame, but the inherent disconnect between the ineffable external nature of reality and our internal perceptions.

Moon Sets In The West, Sun Rises In The East

The approach to duality from a Western perspective clashes with different rigor and intent yet harmonizes with similar truths. Kant’s ‘thing-in-itself’ provides intuition from a Western parallel to the ineffable, such as the Tao and Śūnyatā; as well as his noumenon and phenomena. The dynamic relationship between noumenon and phenomena can be likened to a harmony of opposites, similar to that of the Tao (Schonfeld). And a similar deconstruction and skepticism on the power of descriptions, language, or physical senses to ascertain any true reality; perhaps because reality is empty.

Similarly, William James offers fascinating insights in his exploration of the distinctions between faith and skepticism. How belief, and by extension, skepticism, can tangibly change our perception and reality, reducing the probabilities we perceive as possible, and limiting our avenues of action. These opposing forces shape our perception and lived experience in verifiable, genuine ways. This parallels the beginning seeds of dualism and non-dualism in Eastern Philosophy. My belief rests on the middle way still, an agnostic-like perspective, to stay open-minded and absorb the totality of the moment without closing ourselves off to any one belief. I felt faith and skepticism deserved a very special mention because they are some of the most tangibly antagonistic yet real constructs in our language that influence our everyday interaction, whether it is faith in the self, faith in greater realities, or faith in the moment.

Exploring these dualistic understandings becomes an exercise in accepting and transcending these limitations. It points to experiences beyond words and encourages us not to be confined in our constructed ‘houses of being’. Just as one can feel an overwhelming sense of interconnectedness identifying beyond their false self onto the infinite transcendence of reality, one too can feel a cascade of blissful awareness transcending the boundaries of language. No longer obsessed with the superficial naming of things, with the heart of sensory and emotional experience, one’s thoughts can morph from plain enumeration towards a poetic and expansive symphony dealing with pure abstract awareness.

What Is The Sound Of Satori?

A Zen master Linji once exclaimed, "If you meet the Buddha on the road, kill him!" This shocking statement, far from a call to violence, is a kōan – an invitation to a deeper understanding that transcends the literal (“Linji Yixuan”). It is a favorite of mine; I have read many different interpretations, and all of them provoke insight. It beckons for me a deeply personal, and holistic journey toward greater enlightenment.

Kōans are a form of Zen wisdom that challenges our reliance on language and duality. They force us to confront the limitations of our thinking and provide a path to Satori, a moment of profound realization that cannot be captured in words.

Zen recognizes the inherent dualistic friction in language, the way it categorizes and dissects reality. This, while necessary for everyday functioning, can become a barrier to true understanding. Kōans challenge this. Take the paradoxical question, "What is your original face before your mother and father were born?" The mind struggles, but answers lie beyond superficial words and concepts.

The only thing that eludes this almost corruptive touch of language is our personal subjective experience, a type of qualia. This pattern of qualia speaks to another grand truth: to ever experience something in full, to ever experience the totality of a hypothetical unfettered perception, one that is not clouded by the approximations and confounding of distinct things under a misguided umbrella, one must experience it entirely at the moment, under no pretense, under no analysis, under no dissection, under no approximation.

Śūnyatā, the great Zen insight into emptiness, is another such doorway to transcendence. Its translation, ‘emptiness’, provokes a great dualistic conflict between its definitions and its shrouded reality. Is a tight ball of rubber bands empty because nothing is inside, or is it full to the brim with tension, energy, and connections? This emptiness reflects this tensed ball of connections—the realization that all phenomena are interconnected without inherent existence. The teaching of Śūnyatā erases the comfortable dualities we cling to. Like kōans, it challenges our conventional understanding and frees us from the constraints of rigid and constant language. A few esteemed Buddhist academics remark on this nature of our consciousness, observing that the constancy with which we perceive reality—colors, sounds, smells, our being—doesn’t exist (Kaszniak). They claim that “conscious experience is constantly simulating and interdependently enacting our reality." To understand and lead to greater awareness, we need not necessarily look outward for great truths but inward toward our relations.

Kōans are not puzzles to be solved with singular answers but guides to experience unique insight. To grasp a kōan is to let go of grasping, to allow the mind to transcend linguistic analysis for a clearer realization of grand relations. In a moment of satori, as I write this, I realize the profound connection between kōans and the previous paragraph. To intuit a kōan, we must stimulate and enact a missing piece of our reality, recognizing the interdependence that shapes our existence. It's a deeply personal journey that cannot be prescribed for others.

Rather than working within language's constraints, we can acknowledge them and find ways to transcend them. In Zen, language is not purely symbols or noises we make, but a complex dance of personal experiences, pointing in a more precise direction than only words could ascertain. To embody rather than describe, to encompass rather than dissect. Kōans remind us that language is both a lantern and a shadow, illuminating our path while simultaneously casting shadows that can obstruct our view.

In my journey, embracing the profound concepts of the East, in its many forms, Hinduism, Daoism, and Buddhism, all lead me to a great light that is grounded in humility, respect, and compassion. They have fostered a way of living that seeks to honor the multi-dimensional nature of reality, acknowledging that every individual’s subjective perspective adds another dimension to the tensed rubber ball that is reality. To be humbled by the infinite Tao, to feel no sense of superiority or egocentricity from the freeing constraints of Śūnyatā, and to appreciate the personal and deeply meaningful subjective experience of all things with compassion and kindness even when it does not match one’s values.

A Fool’s Guide To Tight Rope Walking

In my own life, I have tried to understand my struggles and flaws through the lens of Eastern Philosophy. I have touched on the conceptualization of the virtues of these disciplines, but I believe it is of great importance to outline the real, practical, and specific ways I can utilize them on my path.

Played by an overabundance of yang, a fiery will to control and grasp, I often found myself caught in intense cycles of desire, attachment, and self-inflicted suffering. I'd grapple with scenarios beyond my control, finding ways to blame myself for them—a kind of masochistic penance. This intensity left me weakened and fragile, forcing me to crash, rest, and embrace yin, only to repeat the cycle.

Breaking this cycle reached a new height when I turned my attention to Zen Buddhism, Confucianism, and Daoism, seeking balance not through words or equations but through direct experience. Meditation can come in many forms and is adaptable to many needs. I use Zazen meditation to quiet the mind away from its linguistic impulses, the chatter of the monkey mind. I avoid trying to digest my momentary reality as a concept or calculation to solve but simply live it as a felt experience. A first quiet step towards breaking free from the cyclical patterns of thought, towards an unfiltered, pure state of being.

Where there was intense compassion, there was intense judgment and frustration with the state of being. The friction between my desires, even if compassionate, and reality. Loving-kindness (mettā) meditation helps sow the seeds of gentleness, and patience. Projecting my capacity for compassion towards myself, and all that exists within myself in the moment. Not allowing my past anger or future concerns to cloud my present moment with bitterness.

Reading more from wise men and engaging in Eastern philosophy. I feel like an intensely pleasing song, a hum of wind chimes, a constant rhythm of crystallization and realization of things known yet incapable of existing in harmony with and actualizing. So many things that I identified with for so long yet did not understand to a true extent; I could say the words balance, middle way, or duality, 500 times, their definitions, and all their connotations, and it would not have helped me reach any greater point of view. I had to feel, experience, and hear wiser words than my repetitions.

Rising with Wisdom, Falling with Grace, Dancing with Rhythm

The lessons from Eastern philosophies like Zen Buddhism, Confucianism, and Daoism, along with Western thinkers like Kant and James, guide me towards a life lived in balance, aware of the dualities yet not confined by them. Through meditation, mindfulness, and a gentle embrace of life's complexities, I have begun to transcend the superficial confines of language, learning to dwell in pure and abstract experience.

The words of Chan Master I-tuan resonate, "Speech is blasphemy, silence a lie. Above speech and silence, there is a way out." As we step beyond the limitations of language and embrace the profound wisdom embedded in the very fabric of our being, we find a way out—a path to a greater harmony that transcends definitions and embraces the infinite complexity of reality. The harmony sought is not a static point but a continuous and dynamic equilibrium. It is not an equation to solve, but a song to sing, a dance to dance, and a tightrope to walk with the grace of understanding and the courage of a fool.
