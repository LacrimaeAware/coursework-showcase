A persuasive speech about holism: understanding needs, relationships and goals as connected parts of a whole.

Read the full speech

Presentation outline and delivery
  1. Introduce holism through the difficulty of balancing several parts of life.
  2. Connect physical, mental and social needs, then contrast a whole-person view with a single goal or label.
  3. Use examples from medicine and psychology to challenge narrow measures of success.
  4. Return to the tightrope image and invite the audience to consider relationships, goals and well-being together.

The tightrope image was part of the physical delivery as well as the script. The instructor described the tightrope opening, purposeful movement, visual aids and a prop. The feedback also notes expressive voice, effective pauses, eye contact and humor, and records a duration of eight minutes and sixteen seconds.

Full speech

Have you ever felt like you were on a tightrope balancing all of life on your finger while trying to not look like an idiot in front of your class? Well, I am very well experienced in failing to balance. Through this experience I’ve discovered Holism, or the balance of the whole. It's a philosophy that we lose insight when we view things in isolation because the whole is greater than the sum of its parts. That famous saying goes back all the way to the Greek philosopher Aristotle in 300 BC. I will go over examples in medicine, and psychology. Examples like the Rat Park experiment which studies drug addiction in rats, the paradox of hedonism which explores the result of pursuing pleasure for pleasure’s sake and cognitive distortions. I will connect all of this with my personal experiences. Whether it's balancing a simple spoon or all that life has to offer, holism is a powerful tool to improve our lives.

To fully grasp holism’s meaning is difficult because it is applied in so many areas. Let’s break it down with real-life examples. Holism transforms medicine from merely treating symptoms to considering the entire person, their physical, mental and emotional health. Psychology utilizes holism to better understand ourselves, looking beyond just diagnostic labels like anxiety or depression. Consider how our social relationships can influence our mental health, which in turn impacts our physical and even fundamental needs like hunger. This holistic view is akin to a familiar concept, Maslow’s hierarchy of needs, where each level contributes to our overall personal development and self-actualization. However, unlike Maslow’s model, which suggests our needs need to be completed one before the other, holism claims that all these aspects are interdependent and develop simultaneously. It’s more of a web of interconnected needs, rather than a hierarchical pyramid. Holism is not something easily defined but ideally emotionally understood, similar to how we intuitively sense when something in our lives feels out of balance but cannot always put it into words.

In this quest to find balance, we often come across complex issues, like addiction. This brings me to the Rat Park Study by psychologist Bruce Alexander. Picture this, rats in a basic cage are given the choice between plain water and drug-laced water. Predictably, they choose the drugs. Now, here's the twist. Put those same rats in a rat paradise, a place filled with toys, mazes, and fellow rats for company, and voila, they prefer plain water. This study turned our understanding of addiction on its head. It's not just about the drugs; it’s about the environment, the community, the whole shebang. A related example, The Paradox of Hedonism, by philosopher Henry Sidgwick, directly exposes the flaw of single-minded pursuit. When we focus on only one aspect of life, such as pleasure or wealth or even achieving an ideal physique, simpler pleasures get lost as a side effect. This explains how counter-intuitively when we stop making something our sole aim it often becomes more obtainable and our life more fulfilled. What about our internal thoughts and feelings? Cognitive distortions, introduced by renowned psychologist Dr. Aaron T. Beck, are a common source of psychological distress. A well known distortion directly opposed to Holism is black and white thinking. You mess up at something, and suddenly you feel like the worst ever. We've all been there. By embracing a holistic view, we start seeing things more in shades of gray. We understand that failing a test doesn’t define our entire being or that being rejected doesn't mean we're unlovable. We are more than just our mistakes or triumphs.

That’s a lot to take in. I want to personally connect my life and understanding of Holism in a more relatable way. I want to share a very recent story about a friend of mine, a medical student in the UK. Picture this, 12-hour study marathons, a sea of flashcards, and enough coffee to fuel a small city. He was all in, academically. But at what cost? His stress levels were so high that he would frequently devolve into just texting and speaking nonsense like “bababa” as his ADHD brain short-circuited. Despite my nudges towards a more balanced life, his eyes were locked on his academic prize. As life often does, unpredictability striked. The UK med schools switched to a pass/fail grading system. My friend felt all those hours, all that effort were for nothing. He started frantically looking into immigrating to the US out of frustration.

This story is a perfect example of how a holistic approach buffers us against life’s unpredictability, often yielding unforeseen long-term benefits. To drive this home even further, a small study from a college, named Union College, analyzed the effects of joining a fraternity. Members overall GPAs dropped by a bit, by 0.25. The kicker, it boosted their lifetime income by 36%. Sounds like a good trade to me. A nice anecdote to showcase the tangible benefits of a balanced approach. This brings me to my next point – stepping out of our comfort zones. The bookworms might cringe at the idea of a fraternity, the artists might roll their eyes at a gym membership, and the athletes might laugh at opening a textbook. But often, it's in our weakest areas where a little effort can bring the most gains. Shifting from a fixed mindset, where we lock ourselves into specific roles, to a growth mindset, where we see ourselves as capable of learning and improving in all areas. It's about seeing challenges as opportunities, and failures as stepping stones.

To crystallize and distill all of this into a single quote is difficult, but I have a favorite that is perfect, a Zen saying from Buddhist Takuan Soho, 'Focus on a single leaf, you'll miss the tree; focus on a single tree, you'll miss the forest.' And I often like to add, ‘focus on a single forest, and you’ll miss the beauty of the entire world.’ In our exploration today, from the Rat Park study the paradox of hedonism, cognitive distortions, to the story of my highly caffeinated friend, we've journeyed through the rich landscape of holism, seeing how life, much like a living dynamic forest, is more than just its individual parts and how powerful holism is as a tool improve our lives. I invite you, in your own lives, to step back from the singular focus that often consumes us. Whether it's grades, career goals, reaching an ideal weight or finding a perfect partner, remember that there isn’t a single path or definition of success. It’s not about avoiding chasing things that matter to us, but making sure to balance them with other aspects, like relationships, simple joys, and mental and physical well-being, this ultimately increases our chances at achieving what we truly want. Taking a page out of the Zen Meditation playbook, I want you to go back to the tightrope in your mind, to visualize each step, each stumble, each stride, I want you to feel a sense of inner harmony between all the parts, to feel your breath, the world around you, and the tingling sensation of the cool air on your skin. Beyond all the complex words, and concepts, this emotional sense of well being and inner joy is what holism is really about. Thank you for listening to me, and joining me on this journey today.
