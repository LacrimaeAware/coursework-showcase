Structural, behavioral and perception-based predictors

The group project asks whether reported happiness is more closely associated with structural factors, behavior, or perceptions of life. It uses the 2024 General Social Survey (GSS) and compares descriptive patterns, multiple linear regression, a marital-status contingency table, and logistic regression.

Structural variables include age, education, income and marital status. Behavioral variables include attendance, television hours and other activities. Perception variables include self-rated health, job satisfaction and financial satisfaction.

Happiness responses and complete cases

The 2024 GSS data contain \(3{,}986\) respondents, of whom \(3{,}954\) have a valid happiness response. The response is reversed to a scale from \(1\), “not too happy,” to \(3\), “very happy.” The binary response groups “pretty happy” and “very happy” together.

Missing values reduce the final modeling sample to \(723\) respondents with all selected variables observed. Both final regressions are unweighted. Their estimates describe this complete-case subset of one survey wave.

Linear and logistic associations

The linear model uses the three-level happiness score. Its adjusted \(R^2\) is \(0.1434\). A slope gives the fitted difference in that score for a one-step increase in a predictor, holding the other model variables fixed.

The logistic model uses the binary response. An odds ratio multiplies the modeled odds of reporting “pretty happy” or “very happy” for the same one-step increase. Health and satisfaction are recoded so that higher values mean better ratings.

Marital status and the binary response

A separate descriptive comparison uses the \(3{,}940\) respondents with valid happiness and marital-status responses. The “pretty happy” or “very happy” share is \(87.3\%\) among married respondents and \(63.0\%\) among separated respondents. Cramér's \(V=0.19\) summarizes the strength of this categorical association.

Ranking probabilities and choosing a threshold

The logistic ROC curve has area \(0.741\), measuring how fitted probabilities rank the two response groups. At a probability threshold of \(0.5\), accuracy is \(81.47\%\); assigning every respondent to the majority group gives \(80.91\%\). These quantities are calculated on the same \(723\) respondents used to fit the model.

Confidence intervals and sample handling

The linear \(95\%\) intervals are \([0.0914,0.2142]\) for financial satisfaction, \([0.0682,0.1706]\) for job satisfaction, and \([0.0403,0.1612]\) for health. Logistic odds-ratio intervals are \([1.360,2.538]\), \([1.374,2.149]\), and \([0.862,1.528]\), respectively.

The final model retains age, education, income, marital status, attendance, television hours, sexual frequency, health, job satisfaction and financial satisfaction. Complete-case filtering omits \(3{,}231\) respondents from the valid-response sample. Trust, helpfulness and fairness items have approximately \(76\%\) missingness and are excluded from the regressions.
