Consecutive composite numbers

Homework 1 · Chapter 4, Problem 24

If \(n\in\mathbb N\) and \(n\ge2\), prove that

\[ n!+2,\quad n!+3,\quad\ldots,\quad n!+n \]

are all composite. Here \(n!=1\cdot2\cdots n\), and a composite number is a positive integer with a divisor strictly between \(1\) and itself.

Factorial divisibility

Fix any integer \(k\) with \(2\le k\le n\). Since \(k\) is one of the factors of \(n!\), there is a positive integer \(c\) such that \(n!=kc\). Consequently,

\[ n!+k=kc+k=k(c+1). \]

Thus \(k\) divides \(n!+k\). Also \(1<k<n!+k\), so this divisor makes \(n!+k\) composite. The argument applies to every \(k\) from \(2\) through \(n\), proving the entire claim.

Sum of cubes

Homework 3 · Section 1.1, Problem 1(e)

Prove that, for every integer \(n\ge1\),

\[ 1^3+2^3+\cdots+n^3 =\left(\frac{n(n+1)}2\right)^2. \]

Induction proves a statement for every positive integer by checking the first case and showing that each case implies the next.

Base case

For \(n=1\), both sides equal \(1\):

\[ 1^3=\left(\frac{1\cdot2}{2}\right)^2=1. \]

Inductive step

Assume that the identity holds for an integer \(k\ge1\). Add \((k+1)^3\) to the sum and substitute the assumed formula:

\[ \begin{aligned} \sum_{j=1}^{k+1}j^3 &=\left(\frac{k(k+1)}2\right)^2+(k+1)^3\\ &=(k+1)^2\left(\frac{k^2}{4}+k+1\right). \end{aligned} \]

The expression in parentheses is a square because \(k^2+4k+4=(k+2)^2\). Therefore,

\[ \begin{aligned} \sum_{j=1}^{k+1}j^3 &=(k+1)^2\frac{(k+2)^2}{4}\\ &=\left(\frac{(k+1)(k+2)}2\right)^2. \end{aligned} \]

This is the required formula with \(k+1\) in place of \(n\). The base case and inductive step prove the identity for all integers \(n\ge1\).
