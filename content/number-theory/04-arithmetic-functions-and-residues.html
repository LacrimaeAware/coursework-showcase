Counting with Euler’s totient function

Homework 8 · Exercise 1

Euler’s totient \(\phi(d)\) counts the integers from \(1\) through \(d\) that are relatively prime to \(d\), including \(\phi(1)=1\). Prove that, for every integer \(n\ge1\),

\[ n=\sum_{d\mid n}\phi(d). \]

The sum runs over all positive divisors \(d\) of \(n\).

Partition by greatest common divisor

For each divisor \(d\) of \(n\), let \(A_d\) contain the integers \(m\) with \(1\le m\le n\) and \(\gcd(m,n)=n/d\).

Every \(m\) belongs to exactly one of these sets: its greatest common divisor with \(n\) determines \(d\). Consequently, the sets are disjoint and together contain all \(n\) integers.

To count \(A_d\), write \(m=(n/d)r\). The bounds on \(m\) become \(1\le r\le d\), and

\[ \begin{aligned} \gcd(m,n) &=\gcd\left(\frac nd r,\frac nd d\right)\\ &=\frac nd\gcd(r,d). \end{aligned} \]

Thus \(\gcd(m,n)=n/d\) exactly when \(\gcd(r,d)=1\). The correspondence between \(m\) and \(r\) is one-to-one, so \(|A_d|=\phi(d)\). Summing the sizes of the disjoint sets gives

\[ n=\sum_{d\mid n}|A_d| =\sum_{d\mid n}\phi(d). \]

Multiplicative orders

For \(n>1\) and \(\gcd(a,n)=1\), the multiplicative order \(\operatorname{ord}_n(a)\) is the least positive exponent \(k\) such that \(a^k\equiv1\pmod n\). For example, the powers of \(2\) modulo \(7\) return to \(1\) for the first time at exponent \(3\):

\[ 2^1\equiv2,\qquad 2^2\equiv4,\qquad 2^3\equiv1\pmod7. \]

Therefore \(\operatorname{ord}_7(2)=3\). The least return exponent records the repeating structure of multiplication modulo \(n\).

The discriminant of a quadratic congruence

Homework 10 · Exercise 1, Section 9.1 Problem 6

Let \(a,b,c\in\mathbb Z\), let \(p\) be an odd prime, and suppose \(\gcd(a,p)=1\). Prove that

\[ ax^2+bx+c\equiv0\pmod p \]

has a solution if and only if the remainder of the discriminant \(D=b^2-4ac\) modulo \(p\) is zero or a quadratic residue. A quadratic residue is a nonzero remainder obtained by squaring an integer modulo \(p\).

Completing the square

Multiply the original congruence by \(4a\) and rearrange:

\[ \begin{aligned} 4a^2x^2+4abx+4ac&\equiv0\pmod p,\\ (2ax+b)^2&\equiv b^2-4ac\pmod p. \end{aligned} \]

If the original congruence has a solution, then \(D\) is congruent to a square. Its remainder modulo \(p\) is therefore zero or a quadratic residue.

Recovering a solution

Conversely, if \(D\equiv0\pmod p\) or its remainder is a quadratic residue, there is an integer \(y\) with \(y^2\equiv D\pmod p\).

Because \(p\) is odd and \(p\nmid a\), the number \(2a\) has an inverse modulo \(p\). Hence the linear congruence

\[ 2ax+b\equiv y\pmod p \]

has a solution \(x\). Squaring it gives \((2ax+b)^2\equiv D\pmod p\). The number \(4a\) is also invertible modulo \(p\), so the completing-square steps can be reversed to obtain

\[ ax^2+bx+c\equiv0\pmod p. \]

This establishes both directions of the equivalence.
